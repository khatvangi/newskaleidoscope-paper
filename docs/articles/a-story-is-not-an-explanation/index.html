A Story Is Not an Explanation

How individual narratives become explanations of entire societies—and why the distinction matters.

Kiran Boggavarapu

Three years ago, at a scientific conference in Vietnam, my former PhD professor approached me with an observation.

“Kiran, you do not know how much India has changed in twenty-five years.”

I asked him what had changed.

“I can no longer go to Kanpur, eat beef, and shout that I have eaten beef.”

I was puzzled. Why would he want to do that? Had he done it before? Was publicly announcing his dinner a custom he had lost, or was he constructing an example to communicate something larger?

I asked him why he would want to visit a place where people might regard eating beef as offensive and make a public announcement of having eaten it. I would not visit a Muslim community, eat pork, and announce the fact to everyone around me. I might disagree with their dietary restrictions. I might even question the beliefs behind them. But what would I establish by deliberately provoking them?

The conversation stayed with me.

My professor was not necessarily wrong about India changing. India has changed, as every society changes. Nor does the question of individual liberty disappear simply because an action is provocative. Freedom includes the freedom to do things that other people find objectionable.

What troubled me was how readily an imagined personal act became an account of a country.

There was no investigation of the extent of the change, its causes, its distribution, or its consequences. There was a memorable scene, and the scene appeared to contain its own explanation.

Later, he told me about an official appeal encouraging people to hug cows. Again, the account arrived as a striking symbol of what India had become.

This time, there was a real document behind the story. On February 6, 2023, the Animal Welfare Board of India issued an appeal encouraging people to observe February 14 as Cow Hug Day. Four days later, the appeal was withdrawn on government instructions. The Indian Express

What should I conclude from this episode? That an Indian government body proposed a cow-hugging observance? Certainly. That the proposal was subsequently withdrawn? Yes. But what does it tell me about the beliefs of India’s population, their actual behavior, or the direction of Indian society?

Those are different questions.

An official document can establish an institutional act. It does not automatically establish the prevalence of the beliefs expressed in it, their acceptance by the population, or their practical consequences. Those connections may exist, but they must be investigated.

I was beginning to recognize a recurring intellectual habit. We select an episode, give it a memorable human form, and allow it to stand for something much larger. The episode may be real. Every detail may be accurate. Yet the explanation built upon it may remain entirely unexamined.

As a physical chemist, I find this transition particularly troubling.

The molecule and the ensemble

Consider a molecule undergoing a conformational transition. We observe its trajectory, record its coordinates, and describe its motion with extraordinary precision.

What have we learned about the thermodynamic system?

We have learned something about one possible trajectory. We have not necessarily learned the equilibrium distribution, the relative populations of conformational states, or the macroscopic properties of the ensemble.

To obtain those properties, we must identify the relevant states, their statistical weights, the constraints under which the system exists, and the relationships among its components.

In statistical mechanics, the ensemble average of an observable A can be written as

⟨A⟩=∑ipiAi\langle A\rangle = \sum_i p_i A_i

where Aᵢ is the value of the observable in state i, and pᵢ is the probability assigned to that state.

But this expression contains assumptions that are easily overlooked.

Which states belong to the ensemble? What determines their probabilities? Is the observable defined at the level of an individual molecule or the entire system? Are the components independent? Does the property depend upon interactions among them?

We cannot simply add every microscopic observation and expect a macroscopic explanation to emerge.

Some properties can be obtained through straightforward summation. Others require distributions, correlations, collective variables, and knowledge of the physical conditions governing the system.

No amount of precision in describing one molecule can compensate for the absence of those relationships.

Human affairs present a related problem, although societies are not thermodynamic ensembles and human beings are not interchangeable molecules. The analogy concerns the logic of inference between levels, not the existence of a common physical law governing molecules and human behavior.

An individual experience may be entirely real without being representative of the experiences of a population. A collective phenomenon may be entirely real without being adequately explained by the experience of any one individual.

Yet much of our public understanding proceeds as though the distinction does not matter.

One lobster, twenty vases

In 2013, Fox News introduced viewers to Jason Greenslate, an unemployed California man receiving food assistance through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP.

The report followed him surfing, shopping, and preparing food with friends. At the supermarket, he purchased sushi and lobster using his food assistance benefits. The lobster was on sale.

The story became part of a wider argument about welfare spending. PolitiFact

The lobster was real. Greenslate really purchased it.

But what did the purchase establish?

It demonstrated that one SNAP recipient bought lobster. It did not establish how frequently recipients purchased expensive seafood, what proportion of benefits were spent on such products, or how much money was being misused.

Indeed, purchasing lobster with SNAP benefits was permitted. The purchase itself was not evidence of fraud. PolitiFact

The distinction becomes especially important when the same footage is broadcast repeatedly.

One person buying lobster appears on television. The image returns. Commentators discuss it. Viewers encounter the same example in different contexts.

The frequency of exposure increases, but the underlying number of observations does not.

Ten broadcasts of one purchase are not ten purchases.

A vivid exception can begin to acquire the familiarity of a general practice without the audience ever learning how prevalent that practice actually is.

Now consider another incident, from the opposite side of a different public controversy.

In April 2003, during the American invasion of Iraq, television news repeatedly showed images of looting in Baghdad. One image showed a person carrying a vase from a building.

At a Pentagon briefing, Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld complained that the same footage was being shown repeatedly. He asked whether there could really be that many vases in the country. Human Rights Watch

Rumsfeld’s objection contained a legitimate statistical question. How many distinct events were being represented? How much of what the audience believed it had seen came from repeated exposure to the same incident?

But identifying that problem did not establish that the looting was insignificant.

The Iraq Museum had suffered substantial losses. Early reports suggesting that approximately 170,000 artifacts had been stolen were later found to be greatly exaggerated. A subsequent investigation estimated that approximately 14,000–15,000 objects had been taken. The losses were real, even though the initial understanding of their magnitude was wrong. American Journal of Archaeology

Here the problem operates in both directions.

Repeated images do not establish the prevalence of an event. But demonstrating that images were repeated does not establish that the larger phenomenon was absent.

One lobster cannot explain an entire welfare program. One vase cannot establish the extent of looting in Baghdad. Nor can the repetition of either image answer the relevant population-level question.

These are not peculiarities of one television network or political tendency. The narrative technique is available to anyone with a compelling example and an audience prepared to interpret it.

Its effectiveness does not depend upon the example being false. In fact, the example’s factual accuracy may make the larger, unsupported inference harder to recognize.

The lobster was real. The looting was real.

The explanations attached to them required additional evidence.

The family and the war

On March 20, 2025, Reuters published a report about Khader Junaid and his family in Gaza.

The family had been staying in a tent beside the ruins of their home in Jabalia. With renewed Israeli military operations, they were forced to move again, eventually finding shelter in a crowded school. The report described their deteriorating living conditions and uncertainty about their future. Reuters report

This is a recognizable form of contemporary journalism. A large conflict is introduced through the experience of an identifiable family. Instead of beginning with military operations, displacement statistics, or humanitarian institutions, the reader encounters people who must live through the consequences.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this approach.

The Junaid family’s experience is not less real because it is personal. Their displacement is a fact about them. Their suffering does not require statistical validation to matter.

But what does the family’s experience establish about Gaza?

If we want to understand the scale of displacement, we need population-level evidence. If we want to understand why people are displaced, we need to investigate the military, geographical, political, and institutional conditions producing displacement.

If we want to understand variation in experience, we need to know who was displaced, where they moved, whether they could return, what resources were available to them, and how these conditions changed over time.

The Junaid family can help us understand what displacement meant to them. Their experience alone cannot answer all these questions.

Independent evidence establishes that their experience was part of a much larger phenomenon. On March 25, 2025, the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs reported that more than 142,000 Palestinians had been newly displaced between March 18 and 23. The office identified displacement orders, destruction of homes and infrastructure, and fear of hostilities among the principal drivers. It also reported that at least 1.9 million people had been internally displaced in Gaza during 2024, many repeatedly. OCHA

Now we have two different kinds of knowledge.

We know something about the Junaid family. We also know something about the distribution and causes of displacement across Gaza.

The two accounts are connected, but they are not interchangeable.

Reuters provided broader context in its report. The problem is not that journalism necessarily stops at the individual, but that the personal account can acquire explanatory authority independent of the evidence connecting it to the larger phenomenon.

A family gives human form to displacement. A population-level investigation establishes its scale. A causal investigation examines how and why the displacement occurred.

A family is not a miniature Palestine. Nor is Palestine simply the sum of the stories journalists happen to collect.

The same applies when the subjects are Israeli families, Ukrainian families, American workers, or victims of crime. The identity of the people involved does not change the inferential problem.

Personal suffering establishes that suffering occurred. It does not, by itself, establish its prevalence or explain the system that produced it.

This distinction is not an argument against empathy. It is an argument against making empathy perform the work of explanation.

The missing relationships

The thermodynamic analogy requires one further qualification.

The danger is not simply that we generalize from too few observations. Even a large collection of individual stories may fail to establish the relevant collective phenomenon.

Imagine collecting a thousand accounts of people who lost their jobs.

We can count them. We can describe their experiences. We can identify recurring themes.

But the collection alone may not explain unemployment.

We need to know how the individuals were selected, the relevant labor-market conditions, the availability of alternative employment, the effects of technology, the relationships between firms and workers, and the geographical distribution of industries.

Some causes operate at the level of individuals. Others operate through relationships, organizations, markets, and public institutions.

A thousand personal accounts cannot replace the investigation of those relationships merely by being numerous.

The number of stories is not the same as the adequacy of the explanation.

The same difficulty arises when we observe the same person repeatedly.

Seeing Jason Greenslate buy lobster twenty times on television does not give us twenty independent observations. Nor would collecting twenty examples selected specifically because they are unusual tell us how common the behavior is.

Before drawing a population-level conclusion, we need a sampling framework, a denominator, and a definition of the phenomenon we are attempting to measure.

Even these may not be sufficient for causal explanation.

A representative sample can establish how widespread a behavior is without establishing why it occurs. Two populations may exhibit similar rates of a behavior for entirely different reasons.

This is why I am reluctant to accept the familiar narrative progression from individual experience to collective explanation.

The passage between levels cannot be accomplished by emotional force, repetition, or the accumulation of stories alone. It requires an account of the relevant relationships.

Those relationships may be straightforward or nonlinear. They may involve interactions, constraints, feedback, or collective organization. We cannot assume in advance that one kind of relationship explains every phenomenon.

Nor should we presume that the collective explanation eliminates the importance of the individual.

Macroscopic conditions shape individual lives. Individual actions can also change collective conditions. Institutions constrain behavior, but they are themselves maintained and altered through human activity.

The levels are neither independent nor interchangeable.

They must be connected through an account of how one influences the other.

Why the formula works

There is a reason this narrative method appears so frequently in public discourse.

A population is difficult to imagine. A family is not.

A distribution has no face. A grieving parent does.

A prolonged institutional process requires attention to multiple causes, conflicting evidence, and changing conditions. An individual narrative gives us a beginning, a sequence of events, identifiable actors, and an emotionally intelligible outcome.

It is a remarkably efficient way to communicate.

Efficiency, however, is not the same as explanatory adequacy.

Communication researchers have studied part of this problem under the heading of exemplification theory. The central concern is how individual examples influence judgments about larger phenomena, including the perceived prevalence and importance of social problems.

In a 1994 study, Hans-Bernd Brosius and Anke Bathelt conducted five experiments varying the presentation of individual examples and statistical information in journalistic stories. They found that examples strongly influenced participants’ perceptions of the distribution of public opinion, while base-rate information had little impact under the tested conditions. Communication Research

A later meta-analysis by Elisabeth Bigsby and colleagues, published in 2019, examined 26 studies involving 5,480 participants. It found that messages featuring exemplars produced stronger persuasive outcomes than messages without them, with a modest average effect. Annals of the International Communication Association

These findings do not establish that every reader responds identically or that every personal narrative misleads. They do provide empirical support for the broader concern: the form in which information is presented can influence judgments independently of its representativeness.

I suspect another mechanism operates alongside this effect.

We rarely approach a story without an existing interpretation of the world. We carry accumulated beliefs about governments, religions, societies, institutions, and other people. These beliefs help us navigate everyday life without reconsidering everything from first principles.

They also create opportunities for error.

When we encounter a familiar narrative, we may recognize it as an instance of something we believe we already understand. The recognition reduces the need to investigate the episode independently.

The story supplies the particular individual. Our existing framework supplies the larger explanation.

In my professor’s account, eating beef in Kanpur was not merely an imagined event. It activated an interpretation of India’s transformation.

In another person’s account, the same episode might activate a different interpretation concerning religious provocation, social norms, or the boundaries of freedom.

Neither interpretation follows automatically from the episode.

The example has become a trigger for an explanation the listener already possesses.

This is where stereotyping enters, not necessarily as hostility toward another group, but as a general cognitive habit. We store simplified representations of the world and retrieve them when a new situation resembles something familiar.

I do it. My professor does it. Journalists do it. Readers do it.

The problem is not that we possess these frameworks. It is that we often stop investigating once a familiar framework has been activated.

The moral difficulty

There is a serious objection to my argument.

Must every personal tragedy be statistically representative before it deserves public attention?

Certainly not.

A rare injustice may deserve investigation precisely because it is rare. A single incident can expose a mechanism that had previously escaped attention. A family can reveal the consequences of an institutional failure that would remain invisible in aggregate data.

Indeed, a single well-documented event may decisively disprove an absolute claim that something never happens.

The moral significance of suffering is not proportional to the number of people experiencing it.

But moral significance, prevalence, and causal explanation are different properties.

A rare event can be morally significant. A widespread event can be poorly understood. A compelling narrative can be factually accurate while supporting an unjustified generalization.

Once we distinguish these questions, the apparent conflict between personal testimony and population-level investigation begins to disappear.

We do not need to choose between the individual and the collective. We need to establish what each can tell us.

The Junaid family deserves to have its experience reported accurately. The displacement of Palestinians in Gaza deserves investigation at the scale at which it occurs. The relationship between the family’s experience and the larger phenomenon deserves an explanation.

One does not diminish the others.

The same discipline applies to the lobster and the vase. It applies to a professor’s account of India and to my own response to that account.

The difficulty is not identifying which political position has the better collection of anecdotes. It is resisting the temptation to treat our preferred anecdotes as explanations while dismissing everyone else’s.

Returning to Vietnam

There is an irony in writing this essay.

I began with a story about my professor in Vietnam. It made a strong impression on me. Years later, I can still recall the conversation.

I could use that experience to write an article about the failings of a particular political culture, religious community, or generation of academics.

But what would authorize that conclusion?

My professor’s remarks are evidence of what he said to me. My interpretation of those remarks is evidence of how I understood him. Neither, by itself, establishes how his community thinks, how academics generally reason, or how people understand India.

If I allowed my encounter to stand for all those things, I would be committing the very error I have spent this essay examining.

That is why the story matters to me.

It did not teach me what India had become. It made me question how readily I, too, allowed a story to become an explanation.

The lesson extends beyond journalism. Scientists are not exempt. We select striking results, construct narratives around them, and sometimes allow the elegance of those narratives to exceed what the evidence establishes.

An experimental observation is not automatically a mechanism. A mechanism established under one set of conditions is not automatically a general law. A correlation is not automatically a causal relationship.

We demand evidence for these transitions in science. We should demand it when we attempt to understand human affairs.

A story tells us something about the world. It may reveal a possibility, identify a problem, introduce a mechanism, or make a collective phenomenon comprehensible through an individual life.

But the story does not carry all its possible explanations within itself.

We must do the additional work of determining what it establishes, what remains unknown, and how its particular circumstances connect to the larger world.

The purpose is not to eliminate narrative. It is to prevent narrative from becoming a substitute for inquiry.

A story is where understanding may begin. It is not where explanation must end.
